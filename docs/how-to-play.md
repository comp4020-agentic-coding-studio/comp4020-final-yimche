# How to play Torchlight

The full rules, as the game plays them today. The same rules, with a short
demo beside the trickiest ones, are under How to play in the game itself.

1. Open the app and give yourself a name to play as a guest. A cookie
   remembers you, so your record is still there when you come back to the
   same browser, but a guest's record is only shown to them and never goes on
   the board. Make an account (a username and password) to get on the board
   and sign in from any device; a guest who makes one keeps their record.
2. Make a new room, or pick one that's open. Each room has a four-letter code
   and its own link: send the link to your friends and they land straight in
   it. Several groups can play at once without seeing each other.
3. The first one into a room is its host. The host picks the mode, and when
   everyone's in presses Start; a three-second countdown runs and a fresh maze
   is built for the round. If the host leaves for good, whoever came in next
   takes over. There are three modes:
   - **Free for all**: everyone against everyone; the last torch standing wins.
   - **Teams**: everyone is dealt at random into Red or Blue, as evenly as the
     numbers allow, and wears their side's colour. Your torch doesn't catch a
     teammate, and bumping one doesn't stun you. When one side is all caught,
     everyone on the other side wins, the caught ones too.
   - **One v one**: the first two in line play and everyone else watches. The
     loser goes to the back of the line, so the winner stays on and the next
     one steps up.
4. Move with <kbd>WASD</kbd> or the arrow keys, or drag on the maze on a phone.
   You speed up and slow down smoothly rather than snapping. Your torch points
   at the mouse, whichever way you walk, so you can back away from someone
   with your light still on them. On a phone, with no mouse, it points where
   you walk. The game fills the screen. The top left corner shows the room
   code, with buttons to copy the room's link and go to other rooms, and how
   many opponents are still in; each catch shows briefly under the banner.
   Its How to play and Scores buttons open one window with a tab for each:
   the rules, and your record beside the best seekers board. How to play
   opens by itself the first time you join. Beside the rules that are easiest
   to get wrong, a short looping demo shows the rule rather than just
   describing it: the catch angle, the head-on stun, the flash and the
   freeze each play out on a small maze, drawn and judged by the same rules
   the game itself uses. With reduced motion on, each holds its key moment
   still, with a button to play it once.
5. You can only see what your torch lights, plus a small circle around you.
   Keep your beam on someone from behind or from the side and they're out.
   Only the quarter in front of them, 45° either side of where they're
   looking, is safe: they can see you coming.
   Every ten seconds the whole maze flashes into view for a moment, and
   everyone still in shows up with it: get your bearings, and don't be caught
   in the open.
6. Walk into someone head-on, or catch each other in your torches at the same
   time, and you both get a short stun: neither of you can catch the other
   until the light comes off and the countdown runs out. Turning tail out of a
   stand-off doesn't hand the other one a free catch.
7. Powerups lie about the maze and glow through the dark, spread out so no
   corner has them all. Walk over one to pick it up; it's yours until you use
   it, with <kbd>Space</kbd> or by tapping the slot at the bottom. You carry
   one at a time: walking over another swaps it in and the old one is gone,
   and a notice says which you lost.
   Whatever you pick up while a speed boost or x-ray torch is running waits
   until that one wears off. Each powerup taken turns up again somewhere else
   in the maze, more than five tiles from anyone, so there's no spot worth
   camping on. There are two more out than there are players, and never
   fewer than one of each kind. They're dealt evenly:
   every kind is always out on the map, and no kind ever has more than one
   more out than any other. The one that turns up in place of a taken powerup
   is whichever kind is now scarcest, so the mix stays even.
   - **Speed**: a few seconds of running faster.
   - **X-ray**: for a few seconds your torch sees through walls. It shows you
     where people are, but it only catches someone in the open, never through
     a wall.
   - **Teleport**: drops you in a random room somewhere ahead of where
     you're facing, a few rooms away, so you steer it by turning first. It
     breaks any beam that was on your back.
   - **Freeze**: use it and one rival, picked at random from everyone
     still in who isn't on your side, is stuck for five seconds wherever they
     are, unable to move or turn their torch. You don't need to see them or
     aim at them. For those five seconds you, and only you, see where they
     stand: they show through the walls and the dark, ringed in ice, and a
     notice names who you froze. The reveal ends when the freeze does, or
     sooner if they're caught or fall, or you are, and using another freeze
     moves it to the new rival. No other player is shown them. The one
     frozen is told a rival may see them, but not who. A frozen player can
     still be caught, and their beam still catches whoever walks into it.
     A freeze only ever picks a real player, never a decoy, but a frozen
     player's decoy shows frozen too, for the same time, so anyone else who
     sees both still can't tell which is which; only you, while you're
     shown where they are, know the real one. With no rival left it doesn't
     go off and stays in your slot. While you're frozen you can't use your
     own powerup until it wears off.
   - **Decoy**: leaves a still copy of you where you stand, facing where you
     face, for eight seconds. To everyone else it looks exactly like you: it
     hides in the dark and shows in a flash like anyone, and nothing marks it
     out. A beam on it does nothing, so whoever stalks it is wasting their
     time while you slip away. Only you see it for what it is, as a faint
     ghost with a dashed ring. Using another replaces it, and it goes if
     you're out.
   - **Blackout**: every rival's torch reaches only half as far for four
     seconds, for seeing and for catching alike, so they can only catch up
     close. Yours keeps its full reach, and teammates' torches are left
     alone. Whoever's dimmed sees their beam shrink and is told why.
8. The dark closes in so a round can't drag on. The maze is drawn as a grid
   of tiles, and a minute in they start falling away into the dark, in
   scattered bits from the edges toward one room near the middle, until only
   a few tiles around it are left a minute later. A tile shakes and glows red
   just before it goes. Step onto a fallen tile and you fall, straight away,
   out of the round. Nobody gets a catch for that. A countdown in the corner
   warns you for the last ten seconds before the floor starts to fall.

Unless the host has picked a map, the maze is sized for the room when the
host presses Start: about fifty rooms of maze per player, so two players
keep running into each other and six have as much space each as two, up to a
size that still fits on a phone. Bigger mazes get more open halls, not just
more corridor.

Maps can be hand-made too, in the map editor: the start screen offers it
beside joining a room, and the host's map picker links to it. You paint walls
and floor on a grid, at any odd size from 11 to 41 each way, or start from a
random maze and change it. Each spot where players and powerups can appear
is marked with a dot, floor sealed off from the rest is washed red, and a
line along the top says whether the map is ready or what's in the way. You
can open any map in the library to start a copy of it. Saved maps go in a
library everyone shares. A map
is 11 to 41 squares each way, an odd number, walled all the way round, with
no floor sealed off from the rest and at least twelve of the marked spots
open, since those are where players start and powerups land. The server
checks every map against these rules before it goes in the library, and says
what's wrong with one that doesn't. Saving a map takes a name, the same as
playing, and the map is listed under it. In the lobby the host can pick any
map in the library, under the mode buttons, and everyone in the room sees
which. A round on a picked map is played on it exactly as drawn, at its own
size, however many turn up; picking Fresh maze goes back to a new maze each
round, sized for the room.

Every map has a look, one of five themes: Night (blue square tiles, the
game's first look), Catacombs (worn flagstones), Garden (mossy brick between
hedges), Manor (oak boards and plum walls) and Foundry (steel grating with a
rust edge). The editor has a theme picker beside the tools, draws the map in
it as you paint, and saves the map with it; the library's pictures show each
map in its theme, named beside it. A round on a picked map is in that map's theme, and a fresh
maze gets one at random each round. The server says which with the round, so
everyone in the room sees the same one. Only the ground changes: the beam,
the dark, the red of a falling tile and every player's colour are the same in
every theme, so a round reads the same whichever you're in. Maps saved
before themes play as Night.

Being caught, or falling, isn't sudden. For a moment the dark stays round
the spot where it happened and fades, while your dot breaks up (or drops
into the hole), and the screen's edge pulses red for a catch. Then a card
says who caught you and whether it was from behind or from the side (or
that the floor gave way), how long you lasted, where you placed and how many
you caught. Put it away with its button, Space, Enter or Escape and watch
the rest of the round; it goes by itself when the next round starts. With
reduced motion on, the fade is plain and the card comes straight up.

Anyone who arrives mid-round watches and joins the next one. When a round
ends, everyone is back in the lobby, wandering the old maze, until the host
starts another.

## Sound

Every sound is made in the browser as you play, with no audio files. Under
the game runs slow music: a low drone and a sparse minor melody, calm in the
lobby, gaining a pulse as the dark closes in. Once the floor actually starts
falling away, the score turns urgent, a driving bassline and a ticking hat
under the drone, and it falls silent when the round ends. Over it come short
cues for each count before lights out, lights
out itself, the flash, picking up and using a powerup, a freeze, a refused
use, a stun, a beam on your back, any catch, being caught, falling, and
winning or losing. You only hear what you'd see or be told anyway: someone
out of sight makes no sound at all. Sound starts with your first key press
or tap. The Sound button in the corner, or M in the maze, turns it off and
on, and your choice is remembered on that device. Off means off: nothing is
started.
